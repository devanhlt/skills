# Playtesting, production, documentation, and evidence

Source basis: Schell chapters 7 and 23-32, especially printed pp. 382-401. The experiment and evidence formats are practical extensions.

## Define what evidence is needed

Schell separates focus groups, QA, usability testing, and playtesting. Select by uncertainty:
- Preference discussion can reveal expectations; it does not establish how a game feels in use.
- QA checks whether behavior matches requirements and locates defects.
- Usability testing examines whether players understand and operate the game.
- Playtesting examines the experience produced by actual play.

A spreadsheet can expose impossible probabilities or runaway resource production. It cannot establish fun. A simulation predicts outcomes under a model; it cannot validate the model's player-behavior assumptions by itself.

## Plan around a question

Use the five planning dimensions from chapter 25: why, who, where, what, and how.

| Dimension | Applied decision |
|---|---|
| Why | Which uncertain claim or design decision does this session address? |
| Who | Which experience levels, motivations, or affected cohorts need representation? |
| Where | What environment permits the behavior being investigated? |
| What | Which actions, reactions, outcomes, and unexpected behaviors will be observed? |
| How | How will observations, comments, and relevant event data be captured? |

Avoid coaching participants through the thing being tested. If facilitation is necessary, record it as intervention rather than evidence of independent comprehension. Watch behavior as well as asking opinions.

Use neutral follow-ups about moments that were confusing, satisfying, boring, or frustrating. Do not ask players to solve the design problem for you. Distinguish their symptoms and preferences from your proposed explanation.

## Define a revision rule

Example: "If participants repeatedly select the wrong target because they cannot distinguish the active mode, revise mode feedback before changing targeting rules."

Choose numerical criteria only when justified by the project and available data. Small exploratory tests help locate problems; they do not produce reliable population-wide retention claims. Preserve observations that contradict the preferred concept.

## Document decisions for their audience

Chapter 24 rejects the idea that a perfect universal document can eliminate iterative design. Use documentation to preserve decisions, reasoning, open questions, and shared understanding.

A brief feature spec can contain purpose, rules, state transitions, feedback, dependencies, examples, and unresolved decisions. Separate current behavior from proposals. Update documents when decisions change.

Use a diagram when relationships are easier to understand spatially. Do not treat an attractive illustration as an implementable specification. Team members need enough context to make consistent small decisions during implementation.

The earlier Stone Librande transcript is not a source for this skill; this documentation guidance is grounded in Schell chapter 24.

## Technology and production

Distinguish technology that enables the core experience from technology that mainly adds presentation. Prototype uncertain capabilities before committing to content that depends on them. Use existing tools and constraints supplied by the user; do not assume a particular engine or pipeline.

Record dependencies and costs that could invalidate the design. A novel mechanic requiring new animation, network behavior, and tooling can be more expensive than its short description suggests.

## Clients, pitches, and commercial context

Use chapters 27-29 when stakeholder alignment or funding is part of the task. Understand the underlying goal behind a requested feature before accepting or rejecting the suggested implementation.

A pitch should communicate the experience, intended audience, differentiator, evidence, production feasibility, and a clear next decision. Tailor it to the stakeholder. Do not invent market sizes, prices, current platform terms, or revenue projections from the book's historical examples.

A research or hobby design need not have a monetization strategy. If commercial analysis is requested, use current verified facts or explicitly labeled assumptions.

## Player impact and designer motivation

Chapters 30-32 ask the designer to consider how games affect players, responsibility for those effects, and personal purpose. Translate that into the actual design: whether a reward supports enjoyable voluntary play, whether consequences are understandable, and whether the system delivers what it promises.

Distinguish an author's philosophical or psychological argument from established contemporary research. Verify specific scientific claims if the user needs a research-backed conclusion rather than practitioner design guidance.
